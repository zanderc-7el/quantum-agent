# Research basis and boundaries

This skill combines the user's video framework with the six-paper review and general text-revision methods. The research basis supports the workflow, but it does not establish that any single method is a complete treatment for defensive academic language.

## What the six studies establish

- Crosthwaite, Sun, and Jiang (2026), *The Modern Language Journal*: prompt specificity changes metadiscourse frequency and realization. Explicitly requesting metadiscourse can create overuse and formulaic patterns. The authors recommend treating AI text as an object of critical analysis and training iterative evaluation, revision, and rhetorical reshaping rather than treating it as an expert model.
- Mo, Wang, and Wu (2026), *International Journal of Chinese Language Teaching*: AI and human Chinese abstracts can have partly similar overall metadiscourse frequency while differing in organization, formulaicity, and authorial stance.
- Zhang and Zhang (2025), *Applied Linguistics*: ChatGPT abstracts use more metadiscourse and more text-oriented self-reference than human abstracts.
- Mo and Crosthwaite (2025), *Journal of English for Academic Purposes*: model stance and engagement resources are narrower and more repetitive; hedge frequency alone does not define the problem.
- Jiang and Hyland (2025), *English for Specific Purposes*: ChatGPT essays can be structurally clear while using less interactive, more impersonal language.
- Reinhart et al. (2025), *PNAS*: model writing differs systematically from human writing across genres; larger models do not automatically acquire human-like rhetorical adaptation.

Together, these studies diagnose prompt sensitivity, formulaicity, weak or narrow stance, self-reference, and adaptation problems. They do not provide a validated intervention package that directly measures a reduction in defensive language.

## Methods added as transferable components

- **Self-Refine** (Madaan et al., 2023): generate, obtain feedback, and revise iteratively without additional supervised training. Use it as a revision loop with a defensive-language rubric, not as proof that the style problem is solved.
- **Chain-of-Verification (CoVe)** (Dhuliawala et al., 2023): draft, plan verification questions, answer them independently, and produce a verified response. Use it to prevent unsupported claims from being hidden behind generic caution.
- **RARR** (Gao et al., ACL 2023): research and revise generated text to add attribution and repair unsupported content while preserving the original output where possible. Use it for source-linked claim auditing.
- **Read, Revise, Repeat** (Du et al., 2022): keep a human in the loop so an author can accept or reject each proposed edit. Use this as the final authority for stance and disciplinary meaning.

These methods improve verification or revision quality in their original settings. They do not by themselves prove that defensive language has decreased. The sentence consumer test, claim ledger, and human decision remain the specialized evaluation layer.
